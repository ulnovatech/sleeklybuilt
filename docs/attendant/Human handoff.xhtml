Yes. Human handoff should be treated as an exceptional escalation state, not a customer-service escape hatch.

And the architecture you're describing is very feasible: the website visitor talks to the Attendant, while you receive and respond to conversations from a private mobile operator app, essentially a Messenger/WhatsApp-like interface. The Attendant remains active in the middle, so you don't need to personally watch every conversation.

1. First: define the handoff properly

I would make this a hard policy + state machine, not something the model casually decides.

       ▼

                    │

The important part:

"I can connect you to a human" is not a normal response option.

It must be earned.

 

2. Hard handoff conditions

Your three are correct. I'd define the complete policy as follows.

1. Explicit human request

The strongest trigger.

Examples:

"Let me speak to someone."

"Can I talk to a real person?"

"I want a human."

Don't make the Attendant argue.

Immediately initiate handoff.

 

2. Genuine knowledge failure

Not:

"I don't know what SEO means."

That's recoverable.

But:

The customer asks about a specific business arrangement that isn't in the knowledge base, and answering incorrectly could affect the sale.

Then:

UNKNOWN + MATERIAL IMPORTANCE

             ↓

          HANDOFF

The Attendant should not bluff.

 

3. Material business decision outside its authority

For example:

"Can you give us a 40% discount?"

or:

"Can you restructure the payment arrangement?"

or:

"Can you make an exception to your normal contract terms?"

The Attendant can explain normal policy.

But it cannot invent authority.

"That's something I'd need to have you discuss with us directly."

Then handoff.

 

4. High-value / high-consequence situation

I'd add this carefully.

Not:

customer wants to buy → human.

That's terrible automation.

Instead:

The customer is making a consequential decision where the system lacks authority or sufficient information.

Examples:

  • •unusual custom project 

  • •substantial custom quotation 

  • •complex integration 

  • •unusual contractual requirement 

  • •major scope negotiation 

  • •enterprise arrangement 

  • •unusual payment arrangement 

The Attendant can qualify and prepare the case before handing over.

 

5. Repeated failure

This is an important one.

If the Attendant has attempted to resolve something and repeatedly failed:

attempt 1 → clarify

attempt 2 → retrieve / reason

attempt 3 → still unresolved

                    ↓

                 HANDOFF

Don't let it endlessly loop.

The customer should never have to fight the AI.

 

6. Customer frustration

This needs to be handled carefully.

Frustration alone should not automatically summon you.

But if the customer is clearly frustrated and the Attendant is failing to resolve the issue, escalation becomes appropriate.

Example:

"I've already explained this three times."

That's a strong signal.

The system should stop trying to be clever.

 

7. Safety / sensitive situations

Any business operation involving something the Attendant is explicitly not authorized to handle should escalate.

This is policy-driven rather than model-driven.

 

3. What should NOT trigger handoff

This is just as important.

Do not hand off because:

  • •the customer asks a difficult question 

  • •the customer asks about pricing 

  • •the customer is considering packages 

  • •the customer needs recommendations 

  • •the customer asks technical questions 

  • •the customer asks for clarification 

  • •the customer disagrees with a recommendation 

  • •the Attendant doesn't immediately know something but can retrieve it 

  • •the customer asks about an existing supported order 

  • •the customer asks for a human "just in case" 

The system should be confidently autonomous.

 

4. The best handoff isn't simply "human joined"

The Attendant should prepare the human.

Suppose the visitor says:

"I want to talk to someone about building a custom student portal."

Instead of dumping you into the conversation with no context:

HANDOFF PACKAGE

 

Customer:

ABC College

Conversation:

[full transcript]

Then you open your phone and immediately know:

What is happening.

This is extremely important.

7. The most important architectural decision: realtime transport

You mentioned webhooks.

Webhooks are useful, but they aren't really the complete communication mechanism.

You need two things:

Webhooks

For backend events:

Attendant escalated

Order created

Lead created

Payment event

System event

Realtime connection

For live conversation:

Customer message

       ↓

server

       ↓

phone operator

       ↓

human reply

       ↓

server

       ↓

customer browser

For this, you'd typically use WebSockets or a managed realtime service.

Since your existing architecture already uses web technologies, I would keep the communication model:

REST/API

+

WebSocket/realtime

+

webhooks for asynchronous system events

Not try to force everything through webhooks.

8. The conversation server becomes the central authority

This is important.

Don't let:

website ↔ phone

communicate directly.

Use:

             CONVERSATION SERVER

              /              \

             /                \

        WEBSITE              PHONE

The server owns:

  • •conversation 

  • •messages 

  • •participant state 

  • •Attendant state 

  • •human handoff state 

  • •unread state 

  • •tool actions 

  • •delivery status 

  • •presence 

  • •audit history 

This means the Attendant and human are simply two participants in the same conversation.

10. Human takeover needs a state

Something like:

The Attendant stops sending customer-facing responses.

You reply.

When you finish:

HUMAN_ACTIVE

     ↓

AI_RESUMED

The Attendant can take over again.

 

11. But don't make the human manually "release" every time

You could have:

Take over

and later:

Return to Attendant

But there should also be sensible automatic behavior.

For example, after you close the conversation:

"I'll let you know if you need anything else."

Then the Attendant resumes.

However, never resume automatically while the human is actively typing or clearly engaged.

One architectural principle I'd lock

The customer should never know whether the next useful message came from AI or you unless there is a reason to tell them.

Not because we're trying to deceive them — but because the experience should feel continuous.

If the Attendant says:

"I'm bringing someone in to help with the custom scope."

Then you enter naturally.

No:

HUMAN AGENT HAS JOINED THE CHAT 🤖➡️👨

Just:

"Yes, we can handle that. The student payments part is the piece I'd like to understand properly before I quote you."

That's the across-the-desk experience we're building.

And your handoff policy should be hard enough that you rarely see it. The success metric isn't "how many conversations reach a human"; it's how many conversations the Attendant resolves correctly without needing you, while making the few escalations genuinely worth your attention.
