Why this matters for your Attendant

Imagine:

"What happens if I cancel halfway through?"

The Attendant shouldn't invent an answer.

It should retrieve the actual cancellation policy and answer concisely.

"If work has already started, the amount refundable depends on the stage reached. Our terms spell out how that is handled."

If the customer asks:

"Who owns the website after completion?"

It should know the actual IP policy.

If:

"What happens if I don't provide the content on time?"

It should know the delivery/approval policy.

If:

"Can you guarantee Google ranking?"

It should know your actual position and not make promises your company doesn't make.

 

And this is where your "human expert" idea gets even stronger

The human across the desk doesn't need to remember every clause.

They know:

"Our standard terms cover that."

They pull the relevant document if necessary.

The Attendant should behave the same way.

Not:

"According to section 7.3(b)(ii) of our Terms..."

unless the client specifically wants that.

Instead:

"Yes. That's covered in our standard terms. Once the agreed work is completed and paid for, the relevant ownership rights transfer as specified there."

Then, if they want detail:

"I can show you the exact section."

 

Legal questions should have an authority ladder

This is particularly important.

The Attendant should classify answers into:

A — Safe to answer

Straightforward company policy.

"Our Business Basic package costs UGX 400,000."

B — Answer from authoritative document

"Our standard cancellation terms say..."

C — Explain but don't interpret

For something complicated:

"Our agreement covers this, but I don't want to interpret a contractual issue incorrectly. I'll have someone review it with you."

D — Human required

If the customer wants:

  • •contract modification 

  • •special liability terms 

  • •unusual warranties 

  • •indemnification 

  • •ownership exceptions 

  • •bespoke payment/legal arrangements 

  • •disputes 

  • •legal interpretation beyond the company's documented position 

Human handoff.

This fits perfectly into the escalation architecture we just designed.

There's another important category: operational truth

You mentioned "legal, not in the corporate sense."

Exactly.

I'd actually call the whole layer:

Company Truth

Because it includes more than legal documents.

The Attendant should know:

What we sell

Packages, services, pricing.

What we actually deliver

Real capabilities.

How we work

Process, timelines, revisions, approvals.

What we don't promise

SEO rankings, impossible deadlines, unsupported functionality, etc.

What clients agree to

Terms, payment, ownership, cancellation.

What we can negotiate

Discount boundaries, custom scope, payment arrangements.

What requires a human

Legal exceptions, unusual commitments, major custom deals.

 

And don't let the Attendant quote legalese unless asked

This is important for your personality.

Client:

"Do you have terms?"

Attendant:

"Yes. We have standard terms covering payment, delivery, revisions, cancellations and ownership. I'll point you to them."

Client:

"What's your cancellation policy?"

Attendant:

"If you've already paid and work has started, the treatment depends on how far the project has progressed. Our terms lay out the details."

Client:

"Show me the exact clause."

Then give them the document/section.

The Attendant should be plain-English first, document-precise second.

 

One caution

Because these are actual legal/contractual documents, I would not let the AI author the company's final legal terms autonomously.

You create/review the authoritative documents.

Then the Attendant treats them as:

ground truth

rather than generating its own interpretation.

And for Uganda-specific legal requirements or genuinely consequential legal matters, you should have the final documents reviewed appropriately rather than treating the model as your lawyer.

Not all of them. I would split the company documents into three visibility levels.

1. Public — customers can freely view

These should be linked from the website and Attendant:

  • •Terms of Service / Terms & Conditions 

  • •Privacy Policy 

  • •Refund & Cancellation Policy 

  • •Payment Terms 

  • •Delivery / Project Process 

  • •Support & Maintenance Policy 

  • •Intellectual Property / Ownership Policy 

  • •Cookie Policy, if applicable 

  • •Acceptable Use / Website Content Policy, if relevant 

These are the documents a legality-oriented client may reasonably want to inspect.

The Attendant can say:

"Yes, that's covered in our terms. I'll take you to the relevant section."

And ideally deep-link directly to the section, rather than dumping them onto a PDF.

 

2. Client-facing but controlled

These aren't necessarily public website documents, but can be shown to an actual prospect/client when relevant:

  • •Detailed package specifications 

  • •Project onboarding guide 

  • •Client responsibilities 

  • •Content submission guide 

  • •Revision/approval process 

  • •Website handover guide 

  • •Maintenance/support guide 

  • •Custom project scope framework 

  • •Proposal / quotation documents 

  • •Project-specific agreement 

For example:

"You'll need to provide the logo, photos and course information before we start. Here's exactly what we need from you."

The Attendant can surface the relevant guide.

 

3. Internal only — never expose

This is where I'd be strict.

  • •Internal pricing strategy 

  • •Discount limits 

  • •Negotiation rules 

  • •Sales playbook 

  • •Lead scoring 

  • •Competitor analysis 

  • •Internal margins/costs 

  • •Escalation rules 

  • •Attendant authority matrix 

  • •Internal prompts/rules 

  • •Model instructions 

  • •Research methodology 

  • •Internal client assessments 

  • •Private company notes 

  • •Internal operational manuals 

  • •Human escalation criteria 

  • •Anything containing another client's information 

The Attendant may use these internally, but must never reveal them.

For example, if someone asks:

"What's your maximum discount?"

It shouldn't expose an internal rule.

It should say:

"Discounts depend on the project and scope. I can have someone discuss that with you."

That may trigger the human escalation rule if appropriate.
