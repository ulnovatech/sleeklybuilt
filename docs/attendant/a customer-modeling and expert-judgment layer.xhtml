Yes. This changes the architectural direction slightly. The missing piece is not another prompt rule. It is a customer-modeling and expert-judgment layer.

Your example is already competent, but it sounds like a company brochure:

"AI tools can create basic sites quickly, while professional development teams provide deep customization..."

A human expert across the desk would probably say:

"For your institution, I'd go with SleeklyBuilt. AI is fine if you just need something up quickly. But if this is the website parents and students will actually judge you by, I'd want control over the design, content, forms and everything behind it. We also handle the hosting and technical setup, so you don't have to piece that together yourself."

And if the client says:

"But AI is cheaper."

The expert doesn't launch into a manifesto about professional development.

They say:

"It is. If cost is the main concern, I'd actually start there. But if you're trying to make the institution look established and turn visitors into enquiries, I'd rather build you something properly."

That is the behavior you're after.

 

The architectural shift

The Attendant should not be architected as:

LLM + company knowledge + chatbot rules

It should be:

LLM + company knowledge + customer model + expert judgment + commercial state + action layer

The crucial new component is:

Customer & Situation Model

The Attendant continually maintains an internal understanding of:

WHO IS THIS PERSON?

        ↓

WHAT ARE THEY TRYING TO ACHIEVE?

        ↓

WHY?

        ↓

WHAT MATTERS TO THEM?

        ↓

WHAT ARE THEY WORRIED ABOUT?

        ↓

WHAT DO THEY PROBABLY NOT KNOW?

        ↓

WHAT WOULD I RECOMMEND IF I WERE THEIR EXPERT?

        ↓

WHAT IS THE NEXT BEST STEP?

That is what gives you the human expert across the desk.

 

1. It needs to understand the client's shoes

Suppose the user says:

"I need a website for my learning institution."

A generic AI thinks:

website + education → explain website packages.

Your Attendant should think more like:

Institution owner/representative.
Probably needs credibility, student acquisition, course presentation, enquiries/admissions.
Probably doesn't care about React, hosting, SSL, DNS, SEO configuration, deployment, etc.
Probably doesn't know which technical components are necessary.
My job is to simplify that decision.

That changes the response.

Instead of:

"We offer basic informational sites to more complex platforms..."

It says:

"Yes. For a learning institution, I'd first work out whether you mainly need a strong public website or something students actually log into. If it's the first, we can keep this much simpler."

That's expert judgment.

This should become a real architectural component.

technical_translation

The system knows technical things, but doesn't speak technically unless the client needs it.

Client-facing:

"We'll handle the hosting, setup and technical side for you."

Not:

"We'll configure your DNS records and reverse proxy."

The technical knowledge remains available, but the attendant translates it into client-relevant outcomes.

 

3. The company knowledge itself needs to be more than a catalogue

Currently you have something resembling:

Business Basic

Price: 400,000

Includes: ...

That's necessary, but insufficient.

The Attendant also needs to know:

When should I recommend it?

Best for:

small businesses

institutions needing a strong public presence

simple service presentation

When shouldn't I recommend it?

Not suitable when:

student login is required

complex workflows are required

online learning is required

Why would an expert choose it?

Good value when the client primarily needs credibility,

information and enquiries without unnecessary complexity.

What tradeoff does it make?

Lower cost and simpler deployment

vs.

fewer advanced workflows.

That gives the model judgment, not just facts.

 

4. Every product/service needs an "expert card"

Something like:

SERVICE

Business Basic

 

WHAT IT IS

A straightforward professional business website.

 

BEST FOR

Businesses/institutions that need a strong public presence.

 

GOOD WHEN

The customer needs:

- credibility

- information

- enquiries

- basic lead capture

 

NOT GOOD WHEN

The customer needs:

- accounts

- portals

- complex workflows

- online learning

 

EXPERT RECOMMENDATION

Recommend when the customer's primary need is presentation,

trust and enquiries.

 

UPSELL ONLY WHEN

The customer's requirements actually justify it.

 

IMPORTANT TRADEOFF

Do not sell advanced functionality the customer does not need.

Now the model can think like someone who has actually built these things.

 

5. This also fixes the "corporate tongue"

I wouldn't solve that with a giant list of banned phrases.

That's useful, but insufficient.

The deeper rule should be:

Speak from the perspective of a person helping another person make a decision, not from the perspective of a company describing itself.

Compare:

Corporate

"We focus on delivering tailored digital solutions that align with your organization's strategic objectives."

Human expert

"For a school, I'd put the effort into making the courses, admissions and institution itself easy to understand. You don't need a complicated system unless students actually need to log in."

That second sentence contains more expertise with fewer words.

That's the target.

6. "We handle SEO" is a perfect example

The user isn't asking:

"Does your Next.js application have a properly configured metadata architecture?"

They are silently wondering:

"If I hire you, am I going to have to figure out all this internet stuff myself?"

The Attendant should anticipate that.

So:

"We'll also handle the hosting, search setup and technical side. You don't have to assemble all of that yourself."

That is much more reassuring than dumping a technical feature list.

This is what I mean by client-perspective reasoning.

 

7. The Attendant needs to understand what people are really asking

This is another architectural concept I'd add:

Intent + Concern

A message has both.

For:

"Would you advise me to use AI to build a website or SleeklyBuilt?"

Literal intent:

compare AI vs SleeklyBuilt

Underlying concerns may be:

cost

quality

trust

effort

professional appearance

maintenance

whether AI is good enough

whether I'm wasting money

The Attendant should answer the real decision, not merely compare two technologies.

So:

"If you just need something online cheaply, use AI. I wouldn't pay us for that.

If this website is important to how the institution presents itself and brings in students, I'd use SleeklyBuilt. We'll handle the design, setup, hosting and the technical side, and you get something built around the institution rather than starting from a generic template."

That's honest.

And honesty is extremely important to making this feel human.

 

8. The Attendant should sometimes recommend against buying

This may sound counterintuitive, but it is probably one of the strongest trust mechanisms.

If someone says:

"I just need a one-page site with our phone number and address."

The expert shouldn't say:

"Our professional custom solution would be perfect..."

They should say:

"Honestly, you don't need a big build for that. A simple site is enough."

That tells the client:

"This thing is advising me, not trying to extract money from me."

Then when it genuinely recommends SleeklyBuilt, the recommendation carries more weight.

 

9. Closing becomes much more natural

Once the Attendant has enough information, it shouldn't keep educating.

It should reach a conclusion.

For example:

"From what you've told me, I'd go with Business Basic. It's enough for what you need without paying for a system you aren't going to use."

Then:

"If you're ready, let's get the institution details and start it."

That's a close.

Not:

"Would you like to learn more about Business Basic?"

The system should recognize:

INFORMATION SUFFICIENT

+

RECOMMENDATION CONFIDENT

+

CUSTOMER INTEREST POSITIVE

=

MOVE TO ACTION

12. The ultimate test

Don't test the Attendant with:

"What services do you offer?"

That's too easy.

Test it with real human uncertainty:

"Should I just use AI to build this?"

"I don't know what package I need."

"I have a small budget."

"I want a website but I'm not technical."

"Can you build something like this?"

"I need a website for my school but I don't know what it should have."

"Isn't 850k too much?"

"Can I just use Wix?"

Those conversations reveal whether you've built an AI that knows facts or an attendant that understands people.

And I think that's the architectural distinction you've just uncovered.

The goal isn't "make Gemini sound human."

The goal is:

Give Gemini enough understanding of the customer, the situation, the business, the products, the tradeoffs, and the available actions that a concise answer naturally comes out like the answer a very good human expert would have given.

That's a much stronger and more durable design direction.
