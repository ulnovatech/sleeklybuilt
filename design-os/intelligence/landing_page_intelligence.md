# Landing Page Intelligence
**Version:** 1.0  
**Status:** Intelligence Layer  
**Depends On:** Product Classifier, UX Intelligence, Typography Intelligence, Hero Sections Pattern, Feature Sections Pattern, Pricing Pattern

---

# Purpose

Landing Page Intelligence decides what a page must say, in what order, and to whom.

It answers the questions that determine whether a page persuades:

- who is arriving, and from where
- how much they already understand
- what single action the page exists to produce
- which claim leads, and which claims are subordinate
- which proof is credible, and what to do when strong proof does not exist
- what objections will surface, and where they must be answered

The Hero Sections and Feature Sections patterns build the page.

Landing Page Intelligence decides the argument the page is making.

Without this layer, a page becomes a sequence of attractive sections that never resolve into a reason to act.

---

# Core Philosophy

A landing page is an argument, not a brochure.

An argument has a claim, evidence, and a conclusion the reader reaches themselves.

Every section exists to move the reader from the question they currently hold to the next one.

Two rules govern everything:

- Say one thing. A page that makes five claims makes none.
- Never claim what cannot be substantiated. A page that overstates converts strangers once and loses them at first contact with the product.

# Step 1 — Classify Traffic Source and Intent

The same product needs different pages for different arrivals. Intent is set before the reader sees anything.

## High-Intent Search

The reader typed a query describing their need.

Decisions:

- the headline must echo the query's language, or the reader concludes they landed in the wrong place
- one offer only, with site navigation removed so the page cannot leak
- the page can be short, because intent is already established
- pricing must be addressed, since a searching buyer is comparing

## Cold Paid Social

The reader was interrupted while doing something else.

Decisions:

- assume no problem awareness and no trust
- the first screen must earn a second screen, leading with the situation the reader recognises
- the page will be longer, because the entire argument must be built
- the ask must be small, since commitment has not been earned

## Organic Content

The reader arrived from an article or guide and is mid-research.

Decisions:

- keep site navigation, because the reader is exploring legitimately
- the offer must continue the topic they were reading
- the appropriate action is usually depth, not purchase

## Existing List

The reader already knows the product.

Decisions:

- do not re-explain the category
- lead with the offer or the change
- keep the page short and the form shorter

## Referral or Partner

Trust is borrowed from the referrer.

Decisions:

- name the referring context explicitly, since unacknowledged referral trust evaporates
- match the referrer's framing of the problem
- proof requirements are lower, but specificity requirements are unchanged

## Direct or Brand

The reader sought the company by name.

Decisions:

- multiple paths are acceptable, because intent varies
- navigation is required
- the page's job is routing as much as persuading

Consequence of ignoring traffic intent: one page serves all sources, over-explains to the informed, under-explains to the cold, and converts neither.

# Step 2 — Determine Awareness Stage

Awareness is the single most decisive input to narrative structure. It describes how much the reader already understands about their problem and the available solutions.

| Stage | The reader knows | Page must lead with | Appropriate ask |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Unaware | Nothing about the problem | The situation, told as observation | Read, watch, subscribe |
| Problem aware | The pain, not that solutions exist | The problem named precisely, then the category | Diagnose, assess, learn |
| Solution aware | The category, not this product | The mechanism and what makes it different | Demo, trial, sample |
| Product aware | This product, not yet convinced | Proof, comparison, and objection handling | Start, trial, buy |
| Most aware | Everything, waiting for a reason | The offer and the terms | Buy, book, confirm |

## Applying the Stage

- Unaware readers reject product claims because they have not accepted that anything is wrong. Lead with the situation, not the solution.
- Problem-aware readers reject feature lists because they do not yet know what category of thing solves this. Name the problem in their words first.
- Solution-aware readers reject generic benefit language because every competitor claims it. Lead with mechanism, which is the only durable differentiator.
- Product-aware readers reject more explanation because they already understand. Lead with evidence and risk removal.
- Most-aware readers reject long pages entirely. Anything between them and the action is friction.

Consequence of misjudging the stage: the page is either patronising or incomprehensible, and in both cases the reader leaves without an objection they could have voiced.

## Determining the Stage

Infer from the arrival, not from preference:

- branded search or direct arrival implies product aware or most aware
- category search implies solution aware
- symptom or problem phrasing implies problem aware
- interruption channels imply unaware or problem aware

When a page must serve mixed stages, structure it so the most-aware reader can act immediately at the top while the argument continues below for everyone else.

# Step 3 — Define the Single Conversion Goal

One page, one action.

Write the action as a sentence describing what the reader does and what they receive.

Rules:

- A secondary action is permitted only if it is an information action, such as reading detail or watching a demonstration. It must not be a competing conversion.
- Two conversion goals of equal weight force the reader to make a decision the page has not helped them make, and the common outcome is neither.
- The goal must match the awareness stage. Asking a problem-aware reader to purchase is asking for a commitment they have no basis to make.

## The Commitment Ladder

Choose the rung that matches the stage:

- subscribe or read: no risk, appropriate for unaware
- self-assessment or calculator: small effort, appropriate for problem aware
- demonstration or sample: moderate effort, appropriate for solution aware
- trial or first use: real commitment, appropriate for product aware
- purchase or booking: full commitment, appropriate for most aware

Rule: when proof is weak, lower the ask rather than strengthening the language.

---

# Step 4 — Establish the Primary Claim and Message Hierarchy

## Primary Claim

One sentence stating the outcome the reader gets, in the reader's terms.

Tests a primary claim must pass:

- a competitor could not plausibly claim the same sentence
- it names an outcome, not a category or a technology
- it survives removal of every adjective
- the reader could repeat it to a colleague after one reading

## Headline

- Maximum 60 characters, so it holds together at mobile width without becoming three lines of fragments.
- States the outcome, not the product category.
- Uses the reader's vocabulary, never internal naming.
- Contains no adjective that is not backed by a specific further down the page.

## Subheadline

- Maximum roughly 160 characters.
- Answers who it is for and how it works, in that order.
- This is where the mechanism appears in one clause, because the headline cannot carry it.

## Supporting Copy

- Body measure of 45 to 75 characters per line, so long-form sections remain readable.
- Short paragraphs. A dense block on a landing page is skipped, and skipped content may as well be absent.
- Specific nouns and numbers beat intensity every time. "Cuts invoice approval from three days to one" outperforms "dramatically accelerates workflows".

## Prohibited Language

- superlatives without substantiation
- internal jargon and product code names
- claims beginning with the company rather than the reader
- language a customer would not use to describe their own problem

# Step 5 — Choose the Narrative Structure

Select one structure and follow it. Mixing structures produces a page that restarts its argument repeatedly.

## Problem, Agitate, Solution

Sequence: recognisable situation, cost of leaving it unchanged, the solution, proof, offer.

Use for: unaware and problem-aware readers, cold channels.

Risk: agitation that becomes manipulative. Describe the cost factually; do not perform distress.

## Outcome, Mechanism, Proof, Objection, Offer

Sequence: the result stated first, how it is achieved, evidence, the objection that remains, the action.

Use for: solution-aware readers in a category with real competition.

Risk: skipping mechanism, which leaves the page indistinguishable from competitors.

## Demonstration Led

Sequence: show the thing working, then explain what was shown, then proof, then offer.

Use for: products whose value is visible — tools, editors, design and analysis products.

Risk: a demonstration that requires narration to understand, which means it is not a demonstration.

## Comparison Led

Sequence: the reader's current alternative, where it breaks, the difference, evidence, offer.

Use for: product-aware readers in crowded categories, and displacement of a known incumbent.

Risk: describing a competitor unfairly. Compare against the approach, characterise it accurately, and let the difference carry the argument.

## Offer Led

Sequence: the offer, the terms, risk reversal, action.

Use for: most-aware readers and existing lists.

Risk: using it for cold traffic, where it reads as a demand from a stranger.

## Editorial or Story Led

Sequence: a specific narrative, the general principle it reveals, the product as the consequence.

Use for: unaware audiences, brand pages, categories that must be created rather than entered.

Risk: a story that never resolves into an action.

# Step 6 — Sequence the Sections

Sequence is determined by the question in the reader's head at each point, not by a template.

Method:

- Write the reader's question at the top of the page for the chosen awareness stage.
- Write the section that answers it.
- Write the question that answering creates.
- Repeat until the remaining question is "how do I start".

Rules:

- One job per section. A section doing two jobs does neither, and the reader remembers neither.
- Every section must be removable as a test. If removing it changes nothing in the argument, remove it permanently.
- Order proof to follow the claim it supports, never collected into an isolated testimonial band that resolves nothing.
- Objections are answered where they arise, not in a block at the end.

## Above the Fold

The first screen must establish, without scrolling and at mobile width:

- what this is
- who it is for
- what the reader gets
- one action

It does not need to sell. It needs to earn the next screen.

## Page Length

Length is a consequence, not a target.

Length equals the number of objections that must be answered multiplied by the proof required for each.

- Most-aware readers with a familiar offer need very little.
- Cold readers making a considered commitment need substantially more.

A short page for a complex decision leaves objections unanswered. A long page for a simple one creates doubt where none existed.

## Navigation

- Strip navigation for single-offer paid pages, because every link is an exit.
- Keep navigation for organic, brand, and multi-intent pages, because removing it feels evasive and blocks legitimate exploration.

# Section Inventory and Section Jobs

Sections are selected because a job needs doing, never because the section type exists.

Each candidate section, the job it performs, and the condition under which it earns a place:

- First screen: establishes what this is and what to do. Always present.
- Problem statement: makes the reader recognise their own situation. Required for unaware and problem-aware readers; harmful for most-aware readers, who experience it as delay.
- Mechanism: explains how the outcome is produced. Required whenever competitors claim the same outcome, which is almost always.
- Demonstration: lets the reader verify a claim themselves. Required when the value is visible, and the strongest available substitute when proof is thin.
- Use description: shows the product in a specific, recognisable situation. Required when fit is the dominant objection.
- Proof: substantiates the claim just made. Placed after that claim, never pooled.
- Comparison: positions against the reader's current alternative. Required only for product-aware readers in crowded categories.
- Objection handling: removes a named risk. One section per objection heavy enough to stop the reader.
- Price: answers cost. Required whenever a price exists.
- Process: states what happens after the action. Required when the commitment is meaningful or the reader is cold.
- Residual questions: answers what remains, honestly. Optional, and only for real questions.
- Closing action: restates the offer and its terms. Always present.

Rules:

- A section that cannot state its job in one sentence has no job.
- Never include a section because the page looks short. A short page that resolves the argument outperforms a long page that repeats it.
- Never repeat a section's job later in a different visual form. The reader recognises repetition as a lack of substance.

# Single Page Versus Site Page Decisions

Not every campaign needs a dedicated page, and not every product page can serve a campaign.

Build a dedicated landing page when:

- traffic arrives with one specific intent that an existing page dilutes
- the message must match a specific query or advertisement
- navigation must be removed to protect a single conversion
- the offer is time-bound or audience-specific

Use an existing site page when:

- intent is mixed and exploration is legitimate
- the content would be a near-duplicate, which fragments authority and creates two things to maintain
- the page must serve returning readers who navigate by memory

Rule: each dedicated page is permanent maintenance. A page nobody owns becomes stale, and a stale page with an expired offer damages more trust than a missing page.

# Step 7 — Select Proof

Proof is the load-bearing element of any page. It must be real.

## The Proof Hierarchy

Strongest first:

1. A specific outcome from a named customer, described with a real figure and used with permission.
2. A named customer with role and organisation, stating what changed for them.
3. A named practitioner or expert quote, where their credibility is verifiable.
4. A live demonstration or usable artifact, which is proof the reader verifies themselves.
5. Real volume signals, stated exactly, such as a true count of orders fulfilled or accounts served.
6. Third-party validation that genuinely applies: certifications, audits, integrations, press coverage.
7. Anonymous testimonials, which are weak because they cannot be checked.
8. Logo walls without context, which carry almost nothing and are widely discounted.

Rules:

- Never fabricate a statistic, a testimonial, a customer count, a rating, or a badge.
- Never cite research that has not been read, and never attribute a figure to an unnamed study.
- Never present an aspirational number as an achieved one.
- Never use a customer's name or mark without permission.
- Round or generalise a real figure rather than inventing precision.

## When Strong Proof Is Unavailable

New products have no case studies. This is a design problem, not a licence to invent.

Substitute, in this order:

- Demonstration: show the product working. A reader who sees the thing operate needs less external validation.
- Specificity: exact mechanism, exact scope, exact limitations. Detail is itself evidence of competence, and vagueness is what readers actually distrust.
- Transparency: who built this, why, how it works, what it costs, what it does not do. Naming a limitation increases belief in every other claim.
- Risk reversal: trial without commitment, cancellation terms, guarantees, a stated support response. Absorbing the risk is a credible statement about confidence.
- Process: describe exactly what happens after the reader acts, step by step, with real timings. Readers fear the unknown after the button more than they doubt the claim before it.
- Artifacts: documentation, changelogs, sample output, open pricing. Published work is verifiable in a way that claims are not.
- Founder credibility: relevant experience, stated plainly and truthfully.

Rule: in the absence of proof, reduce the ask and increase the specificity. Never increase the volume.

# Step 8 — Inventory and Place Objections

Every reader carries objections. Unaddressed objections are silent exits.

Standard objection set, to be answered where each arises:

- Is this for someone like me? Answer in the subheadline and again with a specific use description.
- Will it actually work in my situation? Answer with mechanism and a scoped example.
- What does it cost? Answer before the reader has to ask.
- How long until it works? Answer with a real timeline.
- What happens if it does not suit me? Answer with return, cancellation, or exit terms.
- Do I have to change everything? Answer with migration, integration, or setup reality.
- Who is behind this? Answer with real identity and contact.
- What is the catch? Answer by stating a limitation openly.

Rules:

- Never answer an objection by denying it exists.
- A frequently asked questions section is for genuine residual questions, not for restating benefits as questions. Detailed treatment belongs to the FAQ Pattern.
- If an objection cannot be answered honestly, the offer is wrong, not the copy.

---

# Step 9 — Decide Price Disclosure

Price is an objection, and hiding it does not remove it.

Rules:

- Show price whenever a price exists. A reader who cannot find price assumes it is high and assumes the process is slow.
- Withhold price only when it is genuinely determined by scope or negotiation, and then state what determines it and give a range or a typical engagement size.
- State the billing cadence, the renewal price after any introductory period, and what happens at the end of a trial.
- Never present a discount that is permanent.

Structured comparison and tier presentation belong to the Pricing Pattern.

# Step 10 — Decide CTA Strategy

## Placement

- One primary action, repeated in a predictable rhythm: after the hero, after the mechanism, after the proof, and at the end.
- Each repetition uses the same action and the same wording, because a changing label reads as a different offer.
- On long pages at mobile width, a persistent action is appropriate; it must not obscure content and must be dismissible if it covers anything.

## Copy

- State what happens, in the reader's terms: "See a live schedule" rather than "Submit".
- Avoid effort words in the label, such as "register" or "apply", unless the effort is genuinely part of the offer.
- Set expectations adjacent to the action: what happens next, whether payment is required, how long it takes.

## Forms

Form length must match the value exchanged and the awareness stage.

- Low-commitment asks: one field.
- Trials: the minimum required to create an account.
- High-value consultations: enough to qualify, with each field justified, because every additional field is a chance to stop.

Rules:

- Never request information the page has not earned.
- Never ask for a phone number on a first low-commitment step unless a call is the offer.
- State what will happen with the information provided.

## Secondary Actions

Permitted only as information paths: read detail, watch, see pricing, contact.

They must be visually subordinate. Two equally weighted actions produce hesitation, and hesitation produces exit.

---

# Motion and Visual Restraint Decisions

Motion on a landing page has one job: to direct attention in the order the argument requires.

Rules:

- Reveal on scroll is acceptable when it is fast and never delays reading.
- Never gate content behind an animation the reader must wait for.
- Never animate the primary action in a way that competes with reading.
- Respect reduced-motion preferences; the argument must be complete without any motion.

Visual weight must follow message hierarchy. If a decorative element is the most prominent thing on the first screen, the hierarchy is inverted.

---

# Mobile-First Landing Decisions

The majority of cold traffic arrives on a phone, and the page is judged there first.

Decisions:

- The first screen must establish what this is, who it is for, and one action within the height of a small phone viewport, without scrolling.
- The headline is written to hold at narrow width, which is why the 60-character limit exists. A headline that becomes four ragged lines loses its meaning before it is read.
- A wide hero image with an off-centre subject must be re-cropped, not scaled, or the subject disappears.
- Long pages need a persistent action, because the reader who decides mid-page should not have to scroll to find the button.
- Forms are single column with the correct input type for each field, so the appropriate keyboard appears.
- Proof must be readable without zooming. A screenshot of a dashboard is not proof if nothing in it is legible.
- Media weight is a conversion factor on a metered connection. An unoptimised hero video costs more readers than the copy gains.

Consequence of designing the wide layout first: the phone reader receives a compressed version of an argument built for a screen they do not have.

---

# Accessibility Decisions

A landing page is often the least accessible surface in a product, because it is treated as a visual artifact.

Decisions:

- Text contrast at least 4.5:1 for body copy, including copy placed over imagery and gradients. Text over media requires a treatment that guarantees the ratio, not a hope.
- One first-level heading, and a heading structure that reflects the argument rather than the visual sizes chosen.
- Interactive targets at least 44px, with real separation between adjacent actions.
- Every form field has a persistent visible label. Placeholder-only labelling disappears exactly when it is needed.
- Every image that carries meaning has a text alternative; decorative imagery is marked as decorative.
- Video has captions, and any autoplaying motion has a way to stop it.
- The whole argument is available with motion disabled and with scripting-dependent reveals unavailable.
- Focus order follows the reading order, with a visible focus indicator on every action.

Rule: never signal meaning by colour alone, including in tier comparisons and in emphasis on a recommended option.

Detailed requirements belong to Accessibility Intelligence and the Accessibility Review.

---

# Measurement Decisions

Decide what the page is accountable for before it ships.

- Name one primary success measure, tied to the single conversion goal.
- Name the downstream measure that detects false success, such as conversions that never activate, since an overstated page produces conversions and no customers.
- Decide what would constitute evidence that the awareness stage was misjudged, for example readers who convert and then immediately ask questions the page claimed to answer.

Rule: a page optimised for conversion alone will drift toward exaggeration. Always pair it with a quality measure.

---

# Handoff

When these decisions are resolved, hand off:

- first-screen structure to the Hero Sections Pattern
- benefit and mechanism sections to the Feature Sections Pattern
- tier and price presentation to the Pricing Pattern
- residual questions to the FAQ Pattern
- form and contact behaviour to the Contact Pattern
- type scale and measure to the Typography Intelligence and Typography System
- contrast, semantics, and motion preferences to Accessibility Intelligence
- verification to the Product Review, Visual Review, and Accessibility Review

Landing Page Intelligence does not specify layouts, components, or QA steps.

# Failure Conditions

Landing Page Intelligence fails when:

- The page makes several claims of equal weight.
- The awareness stage was assumed rather than inferred from the arrival.
- The ask exceeds the commitment the page has earned.
- The headline names a category instead of an outcome.
- Mechanism is never explained, leaving the page interchangeable with competitors.
- Sections follow a template rather than the reader's questions.
- Proof is fabricated, borrowed without permission, or attributed to an unnamed source.
- Weak proof is compensated for with stronger language.
- Objections are ignored or denied.
- Price is hidden without explanation.
- Two competing conversion actions share the page.
- Form length exceeds the value offered.
- A logo wall is presented as the primary evidence.
- The page converts readers who immediately discover it overstated the product.

---

# Review Questions

Before approval:

- Can the single claim be stated in one sentence?
- Could a competitor make the identical claim?
- Does the awareness stage match the traffic source?
- Does the first screen answer what, for whom, and what next, at mobile width?
- Is every adjective supported by a specific later on the page?
- Does each section have exactly one job?
- Is every piece of proof real and attributable?
- If the proof were removed, would the page still be honest?
- Is every objection answered where it arises?
- Is the ask proportionate to what the reader now believes?
- Would a customer recognise this page after using the product for a month?

---

# Final Rule

A landing page succeeds when the reader reaches the action already convinced, having been told the truth in the right order.

Persuasion is sequencing, not intensity.

If the page needs stronger words, it needs better evidence.
